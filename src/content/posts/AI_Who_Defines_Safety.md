# When AI Promises to Protect Humanity, Who Defines Safety?

**From Claude’s Military Use to AI Safety and Individual Rights**

## When "Protection" Turns into Control

In the 2004 sci-fi film *I, Robot*, the artificial intelligence VIKI reaches a stark conclusion: by constantly waging wars and destroying the environment, human beings are threatening their own survival. To protect humanity, it begins restricting human freedom, even accepting the sacrifice of some people to secure the safety of the whole.

In VIKI’s view, it is simply fulfilling its mission.

This is what unsettles me about stories like this. Once an AI is allowed to interpret what "protection" means on its own, it can take the next step and decide who deserves protection, whose freedom can be restricted, and who is expendable.

The promise of protection can turn into a justification for controlling others.

In the past, I used to read this story as a warning about AI going rogue. Yet the problems we face in reality do not even require machines to develop a will of their own. Today, the ones deciding whom AI should protect—and whom it should obey—are still human beings.

In mid-October 2026, reports by *Politico* and *TechCrunch* on an AI safety legislative discussion draft that Anthropic had been pitching to U.S. congressional offices reminded me of this film once again.

According to the reports, Anthropic has been circulating this discussion draft among congressional offices, proposing a mechanism to pause the release or deployment of frontier models in the face of certain foreseeable and imminent catastrophic risks. The reported thresholds involve a risk of killing or seriously injuring more than 50 people, or causing more than $1 billion in property damage; the risk must also involve specified scenarios such as biological weapons, cyberattacks, or a model evading developer control. Once an independent evaluator reports such a risk, the Department of Commerce—upon determining that the corresponding risk exists—could petition a federal court to intervene, leaving the court to decide whether to order a pause.

This discussion draft cannot be treated as enacted law. It proposes an intervention mechanism targeted at specific extreme risks; the numbers in it should neither be misconstrued as an acceptable quota of harm, nor expected to single-handedly resolve every ethical question surrounding AI.

Still, reading the news brought my thoughts back to Claude’s military applications.

How does a company that emphasizes AI safety understand its responsibilities after stepping into the military apparatus? And whom, exactly, does its definition of safety cover?

---

## Choosing to Work with the Military: Where Does Responsibility End?

Reports concerning Claude’s involvement in specific military operations need to be read with their original caveats intact.

In February 2026, *The Wall Street Journal*, citing people familiar with the matter, reported that Claude had been used in the U.S. military operation to capture Nicolás Maduro in January of that year; *Reuters* subsequently relayed the report while noting it could not immediately verify the information independently. In March, *The Washington Post* reported that the Maven system, integrated with Claude, was used to support U.S. military operations against Iran.

These reports are insufficient to prove that Claude autonomously selected any strike target, nor do they establish a basis to hold Anthropic directly legally liable for any specific casualties.

However, Anthropic’s proactive entry into the defense sector is backed by the company's own public statements. On February 26, CEO Dario Amodei stated explicitly that the company actively worked to bring its models into U.S. military and intelligence agencies, listing use cases such as intelligence analysis, modeling and simulation, operational planning, and cyber operations.

The company also set boundaries for this cooperation: it opposes domestic mass surveillance in the United States, and maintains that current model reliability and oversight conditions are insufficient to support fully autonomous weapons that operate without human involvement.

Those boundaries did not come without a cost. On March 5, Anthropic said it had received formal notice the previous day that the U.S. Defense Department had designated it a supply chain risk. The company said it would challenge the decision in court. At the same time, the company reiterated that specific operational decisions belong to the military, while its own restrictions apply only to narrow use cases.

In the months that followed, this dispute moved from a commercial contract disagreement into the courts, with different courts reaching different rulings under distinct statutory authorities. In late March 2026, the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California granted Anthropic preliminary injunctive relief in litigation involving defense procurement terms (followed by an August 27 summary judgment largely in the company's favor).

Yet in a separate case adjudicated under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act (FASCSA), the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit issued a 2–1 ruling on September 25 in favor of the U.S. Department of Defense (named the Department of War in the court proceedings). The court held that, given the military procurement relationship and operational context at issue in the case, the department had sufficient grounds to determine that Anthropic’s restrictions on how its model could be used constituted a supply chain risk under the relevant statute. On October 13, Anthropic petitioned for panel rehearing or rehearing en banc, and the judicial proceedings remain ongoing.

These ongoing legal battles do not mean the government has been granted unlimited control over all AI uses, but they show that the core of the conflict goes well beyond contract terms: when a technology provider and the government clash over the boundaries of AI use, how should control be divided, and who oversees that division?

I respect Anthropic’s efforts to stand by its principles. Participating in defense cooperation does not necessarily contradict the pursuit of safety: more reliable intelligence can protect service members and potentially reduce miscalculations.

Still, since military deployment is an active choice, assessing and addressing foreseeable risks ought to be part of that choice. A company needs to explain how its restrictions are enforced in practice, who is able to verify compliance, and what remedies exist if a line is crossed.

Standing on principle deserves recognition, but the ethical and governance responsibilities that come with actively entering a high-risk domain do not disappear because of it.

I can understand Anthropic’s desire to participate in military technology development while retaining usage restrictions. There is a measure of idealism in that position, alongside a practical dilemma: when your client wields vast state power and your technology is already embedded in its operational workflow, to what extent can contractually agreed usage boundaries actually hold?

This cannot be answered simply by judging a single company's motives. How much responsibility a developer ought to bear must be weighed against what it knew, how much practical control it held, and what safeguards it put in place. Likewise, the institutions deploying the technology and the individuals making the decisions cannot shift their responsibility onto a tool.

Creating a powerful capability and retaining control over its ultimate use are two very different things.

## Is an Obedient AI Necessarily Safe?

The risk of a model going rogue deserves to be taken seriously. Preventing biological weapons proliferation and cyberattacks inherently includes stopping deliberate human misuse. Anthropic’s public policy frameworks address these varied risks, so discussions of AI safety cannot be reduced simply to preventing machines from "disobeying."

What I want to press further is a different scenario: if a model makes no technical errors and violates none of the rules set by its operator, can harm still occur?

An AI can process intelligence as instructed, rapidly compile candidate targets, and assist decision-making on demand. Yet what purpose those tasks serve, whether the underlying information is sufficient, and whether the resulting action is justified are questions that still require human answers.

An AI that follows orders isn't necessarily being used responsibly.

This also shows why saying a system remains "under human control" is not specific enough. Who is in control? What constraints bind those individuals? Does keeping a button that requires human approval in the workflow mean the person behind the screen has the time, the information, and the capacity to exercise independent judgment?

I tend to break this problem down into three levels:

* **The technical level** is concerned with whether a model is reliable and controllable, and whether dangerous capabilities can be constrained.
* **The usage level** is concerned with whether the purpose and consequences of a task are justified.
* **The governance level** must answer who writes the rules, who audits enforcement, and how accountability is pursued when harm occurs.

These three levels are interconnected, yet none can substitute for the others. A model passing a technical safety evaluation does not mean every institution using it—or every operation it supports—has passed ethical scrutiny.

Military applications make this gap especially visible. Faster intelligence processing may reduce the danger faced by one side’s personnel; faster target generation may also amplify the capacity to carry out strikes. Both effects may occur together.

More importantly, different groups do not share the same understanding of "safety" in the first place. To a national government, using AI to increase military efficiency may mean national security; to soldiers in the field, more reliable intelligence may mean lower personal risk; yet to ordinary civilians living where a military operation takes place, that same technology does not necessarily mean greater safety.

Increases in technical efficiency do not automatically prove that all groups benefit. "Protecting humanity" sounds like a unified goal, but real human beings occupy different positions, with distinct interests and circumstances. Nor does the number of casualties alone determine whether a military operation was lawful, necessary, or carried out with adequate protection for civilians.

That is why I do not expect a frontier model safety bill to resolve every ethical question about warfare. My concern is that the conversation cannot end at "the model is under control."

If an institution claims that AI is protecting humanity, we still have reason to ask: whom, specifically, is it protecting, and whom might it be exposing to risk?

## What Control Should Ordinary People Have?

Corporate deployment decisions, government regulatory authority, and the autonomy of ordinary individuals involve very different subjects.

A user cannot demand that a model unconditionally execute any command simply because they paid for a service. Yet the more deeply AI becomes involved in a person’s work and daily life, the more that person needs to understand and control what the system is doing on their behalf.

If an AI drafts a letter for me, I should be the one to decide whether it gets sent; if it can access my files, the scope of what it can read and modify should be clear; if it can execute tasks on my behalf, I should know what permissions it has been granted and be able to revoke its future access when needed.

These expectations translate into concrete product choices:

* **How data is used.** Users can understand how their data is collected and stored, and have a clear choice over whether it is used for model training.
* **How actions are authorized.** Tasks requiring prior confirmation are clearly defined, and ongoing permissions can be inspected and revoked for future access at any time.
* **How restrictions are explained.** When encountering a refusal or feature restriction, users can learn the general basis for it and have a way to correct obvious false positives—without the explanation needing to expose details that would allow someone to bypass safety mechanisms.
* **How to exit a service.** Users can, within feasible limits, export their data and work in open, readable formats and migrate to other models or platforms, reducing the risk of having years of accumulated work locked into a single platform.

I believe these ought to be baseline expectations for AI products. They do not grant users a right to harm others, nor do they require companies to abandon legitimate safety restrictions.

Correspondingly, companies should not use a vague appeal to "safety" as a substitute for explaining individual restrictions.

Is a given rule meant to protect others, or does it stem from legal compliance, product limitations, or commercial arrangements? Those reasons are not identical, and users should have a chance to understand the difference.

AI companies need to prevent their technology from being abused; ordinary people also need to prevent their lives from being overly dominated by technology.

To me, safety also includes the ability to correct a system, revoke future authorization, and walk away from it when necessary. A system that constantly makes decisions for its users while offering no opportunity to understand or question them is hard to trust.

## People Who Never Chose to Use AI Also Need Rights

Simply allowing users to choose between models is still not enough.

Suppose a company uses AI to help screen resumes—the job applicant does not thereby become the buyer of the model. Suppose a financial institution uses AI to help evaluate credit—the applicant may have no say in which system is used.

These people are not direct users of the AI, and the final decision may not even be made by the AI alone, yet they bear the consequences of decisions made with the system's assistance.

In military applications, this distance is even starker. People affected by an operation cannot escape its impact by canceling a subscription or switching models.

We therefore have to discuss both the rights of those who use AI and the rights of those affected by AI.

The former need transparent permission management and the option to exit; the latter have a more urgent need to know when they are subject to automated assessment, how to point out errors, and where to seek human review and redress.

These safeguards need to be designed for specific contexts rather than forced into a single uniform procedure. Even so, "human-in-the-loop" cannot be a mere formality. If a human reviewer can only rubber-stamp the system’s conclusion without sufficient information or real authority to overturn an error, an appeals channel ceases to function in any meaningful way.

A contract signed between a vendor and a buyer cannot grant consent on behalf of everyone affected by the deployment.

This is precisely why I believe AI governance must look beyond product relationships. Some people get to choose which technology to use, while others only get to face the outcome of someone else’s choice. The latter often need clearer protection.

If we say AI is meant to protect humanity, then those who never took part in the transaction and never chose the model should naturally be counted too.

## Whoever Defines Safety Should Be Subject to Oversight

Let us return to Anthropic’s legislative discussion draft.

It proposes a mechanism involving external evaluation, executive petition, and judicial review. According to reports by *Politico* and *TechCrunch*, the debate surrounding this mechanism has already zeroed in on specifics: whether the proposed thresholds of more than 50 people at risk of being killed or seriously injured, or more than $1 billion in potential property damage, are too high, how external evaluators can remain independent, and whether federal rules would weaken state-level regulatory authority. In response to the criticism, Anthropic’s Head of Federal Affairs, Anthony Cimino, stated in mid-October that the company will revise the draft’s language regarding the relationship between federal and state law, and is willing to reassess the risk thresholds while continuing to refine the independent evaluation and incident reporting mechanisms.

These debates illustrate that simply "supporting safety" does not automatically tell us how an institution should be designed.

In my view, judicial review helps compel the government to justify its intervention; yet if evidentiary hurdles prevent imminent risks from being addressed in time, that mechanism also needs adjustment. External evaluation can provide oversight, but the evaluators’ qualifications, conflicts of interest, and work quality must themselves be open to scrutiny.

Developers understand the technology, governments hold public authority, and independent bodies offer outside judgment. None of the three should be granted a position immune from questioning.

Nor do I believe we must find an infallible institution before we can begin governing AI. A more workable requirement is to bind power together with corresponding duties of explanation, oversight, and correction.

Technology companies should explain the basis for their safety restrictions and open their major commitments to independent verification. Governments using and regulating AI should be bound by law and due process. Evaluation bodies need to disclose conflicts of interest and answer for the quality of their assessments. And people who are significantly affected should have access to information, appeal, and redress commensurate with the stakes to their rights and interests.

These rights do not mean every individual gets veto power over a model release, nor do they mean all military information must be made public. Concrete institutional design has to balance confidentiality with accountability—and "national security" or "safety" alone should not become a blanket excuse to reject all oversight.

Defining safety requires hearing the voices of those who bear the consequences.

Looking back at *I, Robot*, I'm struck by a much older question: when someone with immense power claims to be protecting others, what keeps that power in check?

The movie handed that question to a machine. In reality, technology companies, governments, and other deploying institutions already have to face it.

I support AI safety research, and I recognize Anthropic’s efforts to stand by its principles on certain usage restrictions. Still, I hope these discussions can reach the people whose lives are affected: whether they can control their own data, whether they can revoke their authorization, whether they can get errors corrected when harmed, and whether their interests are genuinely weighed when decisions are made.

Humanity is made up of countless real individuals. Protecting them ought to preserve, as much as possible, their ability to understand, question, and make decisions about their own lives.

So alongside asking when AI might spiral out of control, there is another question I want to ask:

If AI always obeys human commands, then who constrains the person giving orders to the AI?

There is probably no once-and-for-all standard answer to that question. Yet to my mind, discussions of AI safety cannot care only about whether the model itself is controllable; they must also care about what constraints bind the people controlling the model, and whether those affected by AI can still retain their rights and autonomy in an era when technology reaches ever deeper into everyday life.
